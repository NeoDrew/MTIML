Section 2

Rademacher Complexity

Quantifying how well a class can fit pure noise.

★ Drew's section to present

This is the centrepiece of Drew's opening — Rademacher complexity and the symmetrisation argument that connects it to the generalisation gap. The presenter view has the spoken script.

Definition

Empirical and population Rademacher complexity

Let $\varepsilon_1, \dots, \varepsilon_m$ be independent Rademacher variables with $\Pr[\varepsilon_i = \pm 1] = 1/2$. The empirical Rademacher complexity of a class $\mathcal H$ on a sample $(z_1, \dots, z_m)$ is $$\widehat{\mathfrak R}_m(\mathcal H) = \mathbb E_{\varepsilon}\!\left[\sup_{h \in \mathcal H}\,\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^m \varepsilon_i\, h(z_i)\right],$$ and $\mathfrak R_m(\mathcal H) = \mathbb E_S[\widehat{\mathfrak R}_m(\mathcal H)]$ denotes its expectation over samples.

Read the inner expression as a correlation: $\frac{1}{m}\sum \varepsilon_i h(z_i)$ measures how well $h$'s outputs line up with the random sign pattern. The supremum picks the single $h$ in the class that best aligns with this particular noise. The expectation averages over fresh draws of data and noise.

Symmetrisation

Symmetrisation argument

A standard argument yields $$\mathbb E_S\!\left[\sup_{h \in \mathcal H}\Bigl(\mathbb E[h(z)] - \tfrac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^m h(z_i)\Bigr)\right] \;\le\; 2\,\mathfrak R_m(\mathcal H),$$ so a bound on $\mathfrak R_m(\mathcal H)$ controls the expected uniform deviation of empirical from population means.

Proof sketch (ghost sample). Introduce an independent copy $S' = (z_1', \dots, z_m')$ of the sample. Then $\mathbb E[h(z)] = \mathbb E_{S'}[\frac{1}{m}\sum h(z_i')]$, so we can rewrite

$$\sup_h\bigl(\mathbb E[h] - \tfrac{1}{m}\sum h(z_i)\bigr) = \sup_h \mathbb E_{S'}\!\left[\tfrac{1}{m}\sum (h(z_i') - h(z_i))\right] \le \mathbb E_{S'}\!\left[\sup_h\tfrac{1}{m}\sum (h(z_i') - h(z_i))\right].$$

Because $z_i$ and $z_i'$ are i.i.d., the difference $h(z_i') - h(z_i)$ is symmetric, so multiplying by $\varepsilon_i \in \{\pm 1\}$ does not change the joint distribution. Splitting the supremum gives two Rademacher terms, summing to $2\,\mathfrak R_m(\mathcal H)$.

Why this matters

Symmetrisation reduces "the worst-case generalisation gap" — an unobservable quantity involving the unknown distribution — to "the Rademacher complexity" — a quantity we can analyse with combinatorial tools. From here, the rest of the poster bounds $\mathfrak R_m(\mathcal F)$.

Interpretation

The Rademacher complexity quantifies the extent to which $\mathcal H$ correlates with random sign patterns: classes capable of fitting pure noise have high complexity, and consequently weak generalisation control (Bartlett & Mendelson, 2002).

Random sign labels are placed above $m$ points. Rademacher complexity = expected best-case correlation of any $h\in\mathcal H$ with this random labelling.
