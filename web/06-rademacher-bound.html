Chapter 6

Rademacher complexity is bounded by the growth function

Apply Massart to label vectors. The trivial bound is useless.

The theorem

Theorem 1.1 (Lecture 11)

Let $\mathcal F \subseteq \{\pm 1\}^{\mathcal X}$ be a class of $\pm 1$-valued functions. Then $$ \mathfrak R_m(\mathcal F) \;\le\; \sqrt{\frac{2\ln \Pi_{\mathcal F}(m)}{m}}. $$

The proof

This is a one-page application of Massart's lemma to the right finite set.

Proof.

Step 1: rewrite Rademacher complexity in terms of label vectors. Recall that $\mathcal F\big|_{x_1^m}$ is the set of label vectors $(f(x_1), \dots, f(x_m))$ as $f$ ranges over $\mathcal F$. Since the inner sum $\sum \varepsilon_i f(x_i)$ depends on $f$ only through these values,

$$ \mathfrak R_m(\mathcal F) = \mathbb{E}_{S, \varepsilon}\!\left[\sup_{f\in\mathcal F}\,\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^m \varepsilon_i f(x_i)\right] = \mathbb{E}_{S, \varepsilon}\!\left[\sup_{a\,\in\,\mathcal F\big|_{x_1^m}}\,\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^m \varepsilon_i\, a_i\right]. $$

Step 2: apply Massart's lemma. The set $\mathcal A = \mathcal F\big|_{x_1^m}$ is finite. Each $a \in \mathcal A$ has $a_i \in \{\pm 1\}$, so $\|a\|_2 = \sqrt m$. By Massart, for each fixed $S$,

$$ \mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}\!\left[\sup_{a\in\mathcal A}\,\frac{1}{m}\sum \varepsilon_i a_i\right] \;\le\; \frac{\sqrt m\,\sqrt{2\ln|\mathcal A|}}{m} = \frac{\sqrt{2\ln|\mathcal F|_{x_1^m}|}}{\sqrt m}. $$

Step 3: replace by the worst-case. Since $|\mathcal F|_{x_1^m}| \le \Pi_{\mathcal F}(m)$ for every choice of $S$, the bound holds pointwise. Take expectation over $S$:

$$ \mathfrak R_m(\mathcal F) \;\le\; \sqrt{\frac{2\ln \Pi_{\mathcal F}(m)}{m}}. $$

That's it. Three steps, each one a straightforward identification or application. The work was done in proving Massart's lemma — this chapter just plugs it in.

Why this matters

Combine with symmetrisation:

$$ \mathbb{E}_S\!\left[\sup_f\bigl(\widehat R(f) - R(f)\bigr)\right] \;\le\; 2\,\mathfrak R_m(\mathcal F) \;\le\; 2\sqrt{\frac{2\ln\Pi_{\mathcal F}(m)}{m}}. $$

So generalisation is controlled by $\Pi_{\mathcal F}(m)$. If we can show that $\Pi_{\mathcal F}(m)$ grows slowly (polynomially, say), we get a useful bound.

The trivial bound and why it's hopeless

Use the always-true inequality $\Pi_{\mathcal F}(m) \le 2^m$:

$$ \mathfrak R_m(\mathcal F) \le \sqrt{\frac{2\ln 2^m}{m}} = \sqrt{2\ln 2} \approx 1.18. $$

This is independent of $m$ — it does not decay. We could collect a million more samples and the bound would not shrink at all. That contradicts our intuition (and our experience) that more data improves generalisation.

What this is telling us

The $2^m$ bound is the right answer for classes that can realise every possible labelling on $m$ points, like a class with infinite VC dimension. For such classes, generalisation is genuinely impossible without additional structure. For everyone else, we need a way to argue that $\Pi_{\mathcal F}(m)$ is much smaller than $2^m$ — and the next two chapters do exactly that.

What "much smaller than 2m" needs to mean

How fast can $\Pi_{\mathcal F}(m)$ grow before our bound becomes vacuous? Suppose $\Pi_{\mathcal F}(m) \le m^d$ for some constant $d$. Then

$$ \mathfrak R_m(\mathcal F) \le \sqrt{\frac{2\ln m^d}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{2 d \ln m}{m}} \;\to\; 0\ \text{as}\ m\to\infty. $$

So polynomial growth is enough. And remarkably, classes that fail to shatter sets beyond a certain size always have polynomial growth — that is the content of Sauer's lemma, coming up.

The dichotomy

Sauer's lemma will tell us that growth functions exhibit only two behaviours: full exponential $2^m$ (when VC dim is infinite) or polynomial $\sim m^d$ (when VC dim is $d$). Nothing in between.

What's next

We need a structural property of $\mathcal F$ that forces $\Pi_{\mathcal F}(m)$ to be polynomial. The right one is the VC dimension: the largest sample size on which the class can still produce all $2^m$ labellings. After we've defined it, Sauer's lemma will deliver the polynomial bound.
