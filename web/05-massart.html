Chapter 5

Massart's finite class lemma

From a finite set of vectors, a square-root entropy bound.

The statement

Lemma — Massart's finite class lemma

Let $\mathcal A \subset \mathbb R^m$ be a finite set of vectors. Define $r = \sup_{a\in\mathcal A}\|a\|_2$. Let $\varepsilon_1, \dots, \varepsilon_m$ be i.i.d. Rademacher variables. Then $$ \mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}\!\left[\sup_{a\in\mathcal A}\,\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^m \varepsilon_i\, a_i\right] \;\le\; \frac{r\sqrt{2\ln|\mathcal A|}}{m}. $$

This lemma is the workhorse of the entire theory. It does not mention $\mathcal F$, growth functions, or VC dimension. It is a purely combinatorial / probabilistic statement about any finite set of vectors.

The bound has two factors that pull in opposite directions. The radius $r$ rewards small vectors (close to the origin in $\ell_2$). The log-cardinality $\ln|\mathcal A|$ punishes large sets — but only logarithmically. Doubling $|\mathcal A|$ adds only $\sqrt{\ln 2}$ to the bound, which is barely anything.

Why this is the right thing

Intuition

You can think of $\sup_a \frac{1}{m}\sum \varepsilon_i a_i$ as the maximum of $|\mathcal A|$ correlated random variables (one per $a$). For Rademacher signs, each individual $\frac{1}{m}\sum \varepsilon_i a_i$ is sub-Gaussian with parameter $\|a\|/m$. The maximum of $N$ sub-Gaussians of parameter $\sigma$ is $O(\sigma \sqrt{\ln N})$. Massart's lemma is exactly that statement, made precise.

The two ingredients we need

Jensen's inequality. For a convex function $\varphi$ and a random variable $X$, $\varphi(\mathbb E X) \le \mathbb E \varphi(X)$. We use this with $\varphi(x) = e^{\lambda x}$ (which is convex for any $\lambda$).

Hoeffding's MGF bound. For Rademacher $\varepsilon \in \{\pm 1\}$ and any $a \in \mathbb R$,

$$ \mathbb{E}\bigl[e^{\lambda \varepsilon a}\bigr] = \frac{e^{\lambda a} + e^{-\lambda a}}{2} = \cosh(\lambda a) \;\le\; e^{\lambda^2 a^2 / 2}. $$

The inequality $\cosh(x) \le e^{x^2/2}$ follows from comparing Taylor series: $\cosh(x) = \sum_{k\ge 0} \frac{x^{2k}}{(2k)!}$ and $e^{x^2/2} = \sum_{k\ge 0} \frac{x^{2k}}{2^k k!}$, term-by-term using $(2k)! \ge 2^k k!$.

The proof

Proof.

Define $$ \mu = \mathbb{E}_\varepsilon\!\left[\sup_{a\in\mathcal A}\sum_{i=1}^m \varepsilon_i\, a_i\right]. $$ We bound the un-normalised version first, then divide by $m$ at the end. Pick any $\lambda > 0$.

Step 1: exponentiate and apply Jensen. Since $e^{\lambda(\cdot)}$ is convex,

$$ e^{\lambda \mu} = e^{\lambda\,\mathbb{E}[\sup\,\sum \varepsilon_i a_i]} \;\le\; \mathbb{E}\!\left[e^{\lambda\,\sup \sum \varepsilon_i a_i}\right] = \mathbb{E}\!\left[\sup_{a\in\mathcal A} e^{\lambda \sum \varepsilon_i a_i}\right]. $$

Step 2: bound the sup by a sum. The supremum of non-negative quantities is at most their sum: $$ \le\; \mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{a\in\mathcal A} e^{\lambda \sum_i \varepsilon_i a_i}\right] = \sum_{a\in\mathcal A}\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[e^{\lambda \sum_i \varepsilon_i a_i}\right]. $$ This is the step that uses finiteness of $\mathcal A$ — without it, we could not pull a sum out.

Step 3: independence + Hoeffding's MGF bound. Since the $\varepsilon_i$ are independent, $$ \mathbb{E}\!\left[e^{\lambda \sum_i \varepsilon_i a_i}\right] = \prod_{i=1}^m \mathbb{E}\bigl[e^{\lambda \varepsilon_i a_i}\bigr] = \prod_{i=1}^m \cosh(\lambda a_i) \;\le\; \prod_{i=1}^m e^{\lambda^2 a_i^2 / 2} = e^{\lambda^2 \|a\|^2/2}. $$ Putting steps 1–3 together and using $\|a\|\le r$:

$$ e^{\lambda \mu} \;\le\; \sum_{a\in\mathcal A} e^{\lambda^2 \|a\|^2/2} \;\le\; |\mathcal A|\,e^{\lambda^2 r^2/2}. $$

Step 4: take logs. Take logs of both sides and divide by $\lambda$: $$ \mu \;\le\; \frac{\ln |\mathcal A|}{\lambda} + \frac{\lambda r^2}{2}. $$

Step 5: optimise $\lambda$. The right-hand side is a sum of two terms, one decreasing and one increasing in $\lambda$. They balance at $$ \lambda = \frac{\sqrt{2\ln|\mathcal A|}}{r}. $$ Substituting back, $$ \mu \;\le\; \frac{\ln|\mathcal A|\,r}{\sqrt{2\ln|\mathcal A|}} + \frac{r}{2}\,\sqrt{2\ln|\mathcal A|} = r\sqrt{2\ln|\mathcal A|}. $$

Divide by $m$.

Reading the proof

Each step does specific work:

  • Step 1 (Jensen) turns an expectation of a max into a max of an expectation of an exponential. The MGF is the natural tool for sub-Gaussian tails.
  • Step 2 (sum bound) sacrifices something — the supremum becomes a sum, blowing up the size by a factor of $|\mathcal A|$. This is why a logarithm of $|\mathcal A|$ shows up in the final bound: we'll cancel one log via the $\ln$ later.
  • Step 3 (Hoeffding MGF) kills the dependence on the actual $a_i$ values, replacing them with $\|a\|^2$ and ultimately $r^2$.
  • Step 5 (optimise λ) is where the square root appears. We have $\ln|\mathcal A|/\lambda + \lambda r^2/2$ — a textbook "sum of $1/\lambda$ and $\lambda$" expression, minimised at $\lambda \propto \sqrt{\ln|\mathcal A|}/r$.

What can break this proof?

  • If $\mathcal A$ is infinite, step 2 fails. To handle infinite sets we need chaining arguments (Dudley's entropy integral) which build a sequence of finite covers.
  • If the $\varepsilon_i$ are not symmetric or not independent, step 3 fails. Both properties matter.

Why we will use it

The set $\mathcal F\big|_{x_1^m}$ is finite (at most $2^m$ vectors). Each binary label vector in $\{\pm 1\}^m$ has $\ell_2$ norm exactly $\sqrt m$. Plugging $r = \sqrt m$ and $|\mathcal A| = \Pi_{\mathcal F}(m)$ into Massart, dividing by $m$, gives $$ \mathfrak R_m(\mathcal F) \le \frac{\sqrt m \,\sqrt{2\ln \Pi_{\mathcal F}(m)}}{m} = \sqrt{\frac{2\ln \Pi_{\mathcal F}(m)}{m}}. $$ That's the bound advertised in the previous chapter, and it is the topic of the next.
