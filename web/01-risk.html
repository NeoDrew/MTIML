Chapter 1

Risk & generalisation

Why training error isn't enough.

Two kinds of risk

We are given a sample $S = \{(x_1,y_1),\dots,(x_m,y_m)\}$ drawn i.i.d. from an unknown distribution $D$ over $\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}$. A predictor is a function $f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}$ from some hypothesis class $\mathcal{F}$. A loss function $\ell(y,y')$ measures how badly $f$ predicted on a single example.

Definition — population & empirical risk

The population risk (sometimes called the true risk or expected risk) of $f$ is $$ R(f) = \mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim D}\bigl[\ell(y, f(x))\bigr]. $$ The empirical risk on the sample $S$ is $$ \widehat R(f) = \frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m} \ell(y_i, f(x_i)). $$

For binary classification with the 0–1 loss, $\ell(y,y') = \mathbf{1}[y\ne y']$. Then $R(f)$ is the probability that $f$ misclassifies a fresh draw, and $\widehat R(f)$ is the fraction of training examples it misclassifies.

The two are very different objects. $R(f)$ is a number we cannot compute — we don't know $D$. $\widehat R(f)$ is a number we can compute, because we have the sample. Statistical learning theory is the study of when one is a useful proxy for the other.

Empirical risk minimisation

The standard recipe is Empirical Risk Minimisation (ERM): pick the classifier in $\mathcal{F}$ that does best on the training sample.

$$ \widehat f \in \arg\min_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\ \widehat R(f). $$

ERM is a fine plan if $\widehat R(f)$ is close to $R(f)$ uniformly across $\mathcal F$, because then the $f$ that wins on the sample also nearly wins on the true distribution. If not, ERM can be disastrous: a class so flexible that it fits any sample perfectly will return $\widehat R(\widehat f) = 0$ while $R(\widehat f)$ stays large. This is overfitting.

The generalisation gap

Definition — generalisation gap

The generalisation gap of $f$ is the difference between empirical and population risk, $$ \widehat R(f) - R(f). $$ We will mostly study its supremum over the class: $$ \sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\bigl(\widehat R(f) - R(f)\bigr). $$

Bounding this supremum is what we actually need. Here is why a per-function bound isn't good enough.

Why uniform convergence is the right goal

Suppose I tell you that for any single fixed $f$, the empirical risk concentrates: with high probability, $|\widehat R(f) - R(f)| \le \varepsilon$. That is true (Hoeffding for bounded losses). But the function ERM picks, $\widehat f$, depends on the sample — it isn't fixed in advance. To say something about $\widehat f$, we need the bound to hold simultaneously for every $f\in\mathcal F$. That is exactly $\sup_f(\widehat R(f) - R(f))$.

The picture: why complexity matters

As the class becomes more flexible, training error monotonically falls but true risk first falls then rises. The vertical gap between the two curves is what we will bound.

A class that is "too small" can't even fit the data — both risks are large. A class that is "too big" memorises the data — training risk is tiny but true risk is back up. Generalisation theory tells us how to formalise the size of the class so we can predict this curve.

The question we will answer

The driving question

Given a hypothesis class $\mathcal F$, when is

$$ \mathbb{E}_S\!\left[\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\bigl(\widehat R(f) - R(f)\bigr)\right] $$

small? In particular: when does it shrink to $0$ as $m\to\infty$?

The answer turns out to be: precisely when $\mathcal F$ has finite VC dimension, a single integer that measures how flexible the class can be on finite samples. Getting from "finite VC dim" to "small generalisation gap" takes most of the rest of this site.
