Chapter 3

Symmetrisation: the master theorem

The ghost-sample trick that turns generalisation into Rademacher complexity.

The statement

Theorem — symmetrisation / master theorem

Let $\mathcal H$ be a class of real-valued functions and let $S = (z_1,\dots,z_m)$ be i.i.d. samples from a distribution. Then $$ \mathbb{E}_S\!\left[\sup_{h\in\mathcal H}\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^m h(z_i)\,-\,\mathbb E[h(z)]\Bigr)\right] \;\le\; 2\,\mathfrak{R}_m(\mathcal H). $$

Read the left-hand side carefully. It is the expected worst-case gap between the empirical average and the true mean of $h$, taken over all $h\in\mathcal H$. That is exactly the generalisation gap (taking $h(z) = \ell(y, f(x))$). The theorem says: this gap is bounded by twice the Rademacher complexity.

So if we can bound $\mathfrak R_m$, we get a generalisation guarantee for free.

The ghost-sample trick

The proof is short but ingenious. Three ideas:

  1. Replace the unknown true mean by an empirical mean on an independent copy of the sample (the "ghost sample").
  2. Both samples are i.i.d. from the same distribution, so the differences $h(z_i) - h(z_i')$ are symmetric — flipping their sign does not change the joint distribution.
  3. Inject a Rademacher sign $\varepsilon_i$ into each term. By symmetry, this leaves the joint distribution unchanged. Now the expression is exactly Rademacher-shaped.

The proof

Proof.

Step 1: introduce the ghost sample. Let $S' = (z_1', \dots, z_m')$ be an independent copy of $S$, drawn from the same distribution. Then for any $h$, $$ \mathbb{E}[h(z)] = \mathbb{E}_{S'}\!\left[\tfrac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^m h(z_i')\right]. $$ Substitute this into the gap:

$$ \sup_h\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{m}\sum h(z_i) - \mathbb{E}[h]\Bigr) = \sup_h \mathbb{E}_{S'}\!\left[\tfrac{1}{m}\sum_i \bigl(h(z_i) - h(z_i')\bigr)\right]. $$

Step 2: pull the supremum inside. The supremum of an expectation is at most the expectation of the supremum (Jensen's inequality applied to the convex function $\sup$):

$$ \sup_h \mathbb{E}_{S'}[\cdots] \le \mathbb{E}_{S'}\!\left[\sup_h\,\tfrac{1}{m}\sum_i \bigl(h(z_i) - h(z_i')\bigr)\right]. $$

Now take the outer expectation over $S$ on both sides; since $\mathbb{E}_S\,\mathbb{E}_{S'} = \mathbb{E}_{S,S'}$,

$$ \mathbb{E}_S\!\left[\sup_h\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{m}\sum h(z_i)-\mathbb{E}[h]\Bigr)\right] \le \mathbb{E}_{S,S'}\!\left[\sup_h\,\tfrac{1}{m}\sum_i \bigl(h(z_i) - h(z_i')\bigr)\right]. $$

Step 3: inject Rademacher signs. Because $z_i$ and $z_i'$ are i.i.d., the random variable $h(z_i) - h(z_i')$ is symmetric: it has the same distribution as $-(h(z_i) - h(z_i')) = h(z_i') - h(z_i)$. So multiplying it by $\varepsilon_i \in \{\pm 1\}$ uniformly at random doesn't change its distribution either:

$$ \bigl(h(z_i) - h(z_i')\bigr) \;\stackrel{d}{=}\; \varepsilon_i\bigl(h(z_i) - h(z_i')\bigr). $$

This holds jointly for all $i$ if we use independent $\varepsilon_i$. So we can introduce signs without changing anything:

$$ \mathbb{E}_{S,S'}\!\left[\sup_h \tfrac{1}{m}\sum_i (h(z_i) - h(z_i'))\right] = \mathbb{E}_{S,S',\varepsilon}\!\left[\sup_h \tfrac{1}{m}\sum_i \varepsilon_i\bigl(h(z_i) - h(z_i')\bigr)\right]. $$

Step 4: split the supremum. Use $\sup_h(a - b) \le \sup_h a + \sup_h(-b) = \sup_h a + \sup_h b$ (the supremum of a difference is at most the sum of the suprema):

$$ \le\,\mathbb{E}_{S,\varepsilon}\!\left[\sup_h \tfrac{1}{m}\sum \varepsilon_i h(z_i)\right] + \mathbb{E}_{S',\varepsilon}\!\left[\sup_h \tfrac{1}{m}\sum (-\varepsilon_i) h(z_i')\right]. $$

Each summand is exactly $\mathfrak R_m(\mathcal H)$ (the second uses that $-\varepsilon_i$ has the same distribution as $\varepsilon_i$). So the total is $2\,\mathfrak R_m(\mathcal H)$.

Why this is called "symmetrisation"

The trick rests entirely on the fact that the difference of two i.i.d. random variables is symmetric around zero — its distribution is unchanged when we negate it. That symmetry is what lets us multiply by Rademacher signs for free, and that is what introduces $\mathfrak R_m$.

If the two samples were not i.i.d., the trick would fail. This is also why the Rademacher complexity is defined with $\pm 1$ signs and not, say, Gaussian noise (though both work; Gaussian gives a different but related quantity called Gaussian complexity).

What we will use

From here on, we treat the master theorem as a black box. To bound the generalisation gap, it suffices to bound $\mathfrak R_m(\mathcal F)$. The rest of the site is dedicated to that bound, via combinatorial properties of $\mathcal F$.

From here, our task is

$$ \text{bound}\quad\mathfrak R_m(\mathcal F)\quad\text{as a function of}\quad m\ \text{and the structure of}\ \mathcal F. $$

The next two chapters describe the structural quantity that will let us do this — the growth function — and turn it into a Rademacher bound via Massart's lemma.
